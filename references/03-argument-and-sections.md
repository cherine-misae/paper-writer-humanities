# Argument and Section Playbook

Structure follows the argument and discipline. The components below are defaults, not a mandatory seven-section mold.

## Argument map

Maintain `argument_map.md` with the question, working thesis, two to five supporting claims, primary evidence, relevant scholarly positions, strongest objection or alternative reading, scope conditions, and unresolved issues. Revise the thesis when evidence requires it.

## Recommended components

### Abstract or executive summary

State the problem, corpus, approach, central argument, and significance. Avoid citations unless the target format permits and needs them.

### Introduction

Define the question and stakes, corpus and scope, intervention in scholarship, appropriately qualified thesis, and movement of the argument. Do not add market statistics or generic novelty claims to create urgency.

### Scholarly context

Organize literature by interpretive problem, concept, debate, or historiographic position—not as a chronological name list. Explain agreements, disagreements, assumptions, and omissions.

### Method, framework, or positionality

Include only when it clarifies how evidence is selected and interpreted. Define theoretical terms and explain edition or translation choices where relevant. No equations or pseudo-technical method section is required.

### Analytical sections

Use at least two analytical movements when the scope supports them. Each has a local question and claim, specific primary evidence, engagement with scholarship, counterpressure, and a clear contribution to the thesis. Use conceptual titles rather than “Analysis 1.”

### Counterarguments and limits

Address the strongest alternative reading. Distinguish evidence limits from lack of space and state exclusions and access constraints.

### Conclusion

Synthesize what the analysis changes about the problem, identify implications, and specify further research grounded in unresolved evidence.

## Discipline-sensitive patterns

- **Literary studies**: question → critical debate → edition/translation → close readings → alternative reading → synthesis.
- **Philosophy**: problem → definitions → argument reconstruction → objections → replies → implications. Separate exegesis from the report's own argument.
- **Intellectual history**: problem → source context → conceptual development → transmission or reception evidence → historiographic implications.
- **Comparative work**: justify comparability before drawing parallels; preserve linguistic, historical, and formal differences.

## Prose rules

Use one argumentative task per paragraph. Do not force first-person plural. Qualify claims by evidence strength. Do not claim novelty until source coverage supports it. Avoid literature dumps and ornamental theory citations. Analyze quotations instead of letting them stand as proof.
